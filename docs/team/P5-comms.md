## What the `.docx` must cover

The portal asks specifically for four things. Structure around them:

1. **The attacks we identified** — the breadth of fraud vectors, not just the ones we built
2. **How our system generates and simulates them**
3. **The detection model, with efficacy results**
4. **Real-world feasibility**

## The cheapest points on the board

Criterion 1 — breadth of attack vectors identified — is scored on **research, not
engineering**. The taxonomy already exists in our background PDF: synthetic identities,
deepfake voice, UPI collect scams, digital-arrest fraud, agentic prompt injection, adversarial
evasion. Writing that up in our own voice earns a scored criterion with zero code.

Do that section first. It's the best return on time available to anyone right now.

## What the story is

One spine, told twice:

> **Threat → Attack → Defense → Result**

Once for the fraud detector. Once for the AI payment assistant. Then joined by one table — the
framework scorecard — showing both, before and after.

That table is the argument. One surface would make this a project. Two very different surfaces
attacked by the same method makes it a *framework*.

## The one rule

**Every number in the walkthrough must come from a file in `artifacts/`.**

Our background PDF promises Kafka, Flink, federated learning, audio deepfake detection and
graph neural networks. **We built none of them.** A mismatch between promised architecture and
demonstrated artifact is the most common way teams lose these.

One Roadmap section, labelled future work. Full credit for mapping the landscape, no claim to
have built it.

The safe check, every time: *"which file in `artifacts/` does this come from, and does it say
`placeholder: false`?"*

Write the sections that depend on missing numbers with a visible marker like
`[[ASR PENDING]]`, and keep a list. Never fill a gap with a plausible-sounding invention.

## Write it in our own voice

The research PDF is 4,000 words with 74 citations and reads as machine-generated. Judges
notice. Use it for threat background — its opening on how GenAI changed the attacker's
economics is genuinely good — and write everything else yourself.

## Include what we didn't do

Have a limitations section: synthetic data, a mock payment agent, a constraint model that is a
judgement call, a detector trained on a subsample.

This feels like weakening the case. It does the opposite. A team that states its own
boundaries reads as one that understands its work.

## What "done" looks like

- `.docx` complete, every number traceable to a `placeholder: false` artifact
- Web prototype URL public, tested in a private browser window
- Repo accessible to judges
- **Published on Kaggle, not saved as draft**
- Confirmation screenshotted

## How to check your agent isn't fooling you

| If it says | Ask |
|---|---|
| "I've written the results section" | *"For each number, name the artifact file it came from."* |
| "Achieves sub-50ms latency" | *"That measurement isn't valid yet. Remove or caveat it."* |
| "Our ASR is X%" | *"There is no ASR in artifacts/. Where did that come from?"* |
| "We use federated learning / detect deepfakes" | *"We didn't build that. Move it to Roadmap."* |
| "The writeup is submitted" | *"Show me it's published, not a draft."* |
